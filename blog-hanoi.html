Hanoi for First-Timers: Old Quarter, Egg Coffee & Crossing the Street

By Adrian Updated September 15, 2026 4 min read

Hanoi throws first-timers in the deep end — motorbikes in every direction, a thousand-year-old street grid, and no obvious rules for crossing the road. Survive the first afternoon and it becomes one of the most rewarding cities in Southeast Asia to just walk around in.

Quick facts

Best time to visit
October–December (mild, dry). Avoid the humid, hot peak of June–August if you can.
Getting around
Old Quarter is walkable; Grab (ride-hailing) for everything further, including bike/scooter options.
Minimum time needed
3 full days, 4–5 with a Ha Long Bay overnight.
Rough daily budget
$20–30/day backpacker, $45–75/day mid-range — one of the cheapest capitals in the region.

1. Crossing the street: the technique that actually works

There's no real gap in Hanoi's motorbike traffic to wait for — it doesn't come. The local technique: step out at a slow, steady, predictable pace, never stop, never run, never change direction suddenly. Drivers are constantly adjusting around pedestrians who move predictably; sudden stops or sprints are what actually cause near-misses. It feels wrong the first time and becomes normal by day two.

2. The Old Quarter: 36 streets, each with a specialty

Hanoi's Old Quarter historically organized itself by guild — each street originally sold one type of good, and many still echo that today: Hang Bac (silver), Hang Ma (paper goods and festival decorations), Hang Gai (silk). You don't need to track this to enjoy wandering, but it explains why the neighborhood feels so densely, specifically commercial rather than generically touristy.

Pro tip Walk the Old Quarter early morning (before 8am) once during your trip — the street-vendor breakfast setup and much lighter traffic show a completely different side of the neighborhood than the evening chaos.

3. Egg coffee and the cafe culture

Egg coffee (cà phê trứng) — whipped egg yolk, condensed milk, and strong Vietnamese coffee — was invented in Hanoi and is worth trying at least once, ideally at a small, old-school cafe rather than a tourist-oriented chain. Giảng Café, one of the originators, is touristy now but still legitimately good. Vietnamese iced coffee (cà phê sữa đá) is the more everyday order if egg coffee isn't your thing.

4. Street food that's worth seeking out specifically

  • Bún chả — grilled pork patties in a sweet-sour broth with rice noodles, Hanoi's signature dish. Small plastic-stool restaurants specializing in just this one dish are usually the best version.
  • Phở — Hanoi-style phở is simpler and lighter than the Saigon version, less garnish, more focus on the broth itself.
  • Bánh mì from a cart, not a sit-down restaurant — the street-cart version is usually fresher and cheaper.

Sit on the small plastic stools if you see locals doing the same at a stall — it's the single best quality signal in Hanoi's street food scene.

5. Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum and the Temple of Literature

The Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum has strict visiting hours (generally mornings only, closed certain days, no bare shoulders/knees, no talking or photography inside) — check current hours before planning your morning around it, since the schedule is specific and changes periodically. The Temple of Literature, Vietnam's first national university, is a calmer, more flexible visit and one of the best-preserved traditional sites in the city.

6. The Ha Long Bay question

Ha Long Bay's limestone karsts are a genuine highlight of northern Vietnam, but it's roughly a 2.5–3.5 hour drive each way from Hanoi, making a single day trip rushed and often disappointing — most of your day goes to transit. An overnight boat cruise (one or two nights) is the significantly better way to experience it if your schedule allows; if not, treat a Ha Long day trip as a "saw it, didn't fully experience it" outcome rather than skip it entirely.

7. Common first-timer mistakes

  • Hesitating or running when crossing the street — counterintuitively more dangerous than the slow, steady approach.
  • Not negotiating cyclo or unmetered taxi fares upfront — agree on a price before getting in, or use Grab to avoid the conversation entirely.
  • Trying Ha Long Bay as a rushed day trip without knowing you're sacrificing most of the actual bay experience to driving time.
  • Skipping the Hanoi–Ho Chi Minh City distinction in planning — they're very different cities nearly 1,000 miles apart, not a quick add-on to each other.
