Kyoto for First-Timers: Temples Without the Crowds & the Bus vs. Train Question

By Adrian Updated September 15, 2026 11 min read

Kyoto has over 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines, which means the real skill isn't finding things to see — it's timing your visits so you're not seeing them through a wall of tour groups and selfie sticks.

Quick facts

Best time to visit
Early April (cherry blossoms) or November (autumn colors) — both stunning and both the most crowded weeks of the year, book well ahead.
Getting around
A mix of bus and train; buses cover more sights but are slower and crowded, trains are faster where routes exist.
Minimum time needed
3 full days for the essentials.
Rough daily budget
$45–65/day budget, $90–150/day mid-range, not counting flights.

1. Fushimi Inari at the right hour

Fushimi Inari Shrine's thousands of red torii gates are free, open 24 hours, and by 10am absolutely packed with tour groups. Go at sunrise, or in the evening after 5pm when most day-trippers have left — both give you genuinely empty stretches of the gate tunnels for photos, plus a completely different, quieter atmosphere. The full hike to the summit takes 2–3 hours round trip; most visitors only do the first 20–30 minutes, so going further also naturally thins the crowd.

2. Arashiyama: earlier than you think

The Arashiyama Bamboo Grove is a short, free walk that looks stunning in photos and, by mid-morning, is a slow-moving crowd of people taking the same photo. Arrive by 8am (the grove itself never closes) to get it close to empty. Pair it with Tenryu-ji Temple right next to the grove, and the Togetsukyo Bridge nearby.

Pro tip Kyoto rewards an early-riser itinerary more than almost any city in Japan — the same sights that are unbearable at noon are genuinely peaceful at 7–8am. Shift your whole schedule earlier if you can.

3. Kinkaku-ji and Ginkaku-ji: gold and silver, different crowds

Kinkaku-ji (the Golden Pavilion) is spectacular and, correspondingly, one of the most crowded single stops in the city — there's a fixed one-way walking path, so there's no real way to avoid the crowd, just time it earlier in the day. Ginkaku-ji (the Silver Pavilion, despite the name, isn't actually silver-covered) is quieter, has a genuinely excellent moss garden, and pairs well with a walk down the Philosopher's Path alongside it.

4. Gion: geisha-district etiquette

Gion, Kyoto's famous geisha district, has had real problems with tourist harassment of geiko and maiko (geisha and apprentice geisha) — chasing them for photos, blocking their path, touching their clothing. Some narrow streets are now off-limits to tourists entirely, with fines for violations, following resident complaints. If you do see one, the basic etiquette: don't approach, don't block their path, and don't photograph without a respectful distance and, ideally, without them noticing you at all. A better way to experience the culture respectfully is booking an actual geisha performance or tea ceremony rather than street-photographing real working geisha.

5. Bus vs. train: how to actually decide

Kyoto's city buses cover almost every major temple but move slowly and get seriously crowded, especially the routes to Kiyomizu-dera and Arashiyama. A one-day or two-day bus pass makes sense if your day is bus-route-heavy; otherwise, mix in trains and short walks where routes allow — the Keihan and Hankyu lines connect several areas faster than the equivalent bus route. Don't default to buses for everything; check each specific route before assuming it's the fastest option.

6. Kiyomizu-dera and the eastern old town

Kiyomizu-dera's wooden stage, built without a single nail, offers one of the best city views in Kyoto. The approach through Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka, preserved old streets lined with traditional shops, is worth slowing down for rather than rushing straight to the temple — go in the late afternoon for softer light and thinner crowds on the approach streets.

7. Common first-timer mistakes

  • Visiting the famous sights at midday — Kyoto's crowd patterns are extreme and predictable; early morning or early evening consistently beats 10am–3pm.
  • Chasing or photographing geisha up close — actively harmful to residents and, on some streets, now finable.
  • Assuming buses are always faster — check the specific route; trains often beat buses on the same journey.
  • Trying to see all of Kyoto's temples — pick 4–5 that genuinely interest you rather than rushing through a dozen at a surface level.
