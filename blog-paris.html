Paris for First-Timers: Arrondissements, the Metro & Skipping the Lines

By Adrian Updated September 14, 2026 11 min read

Paris punishes bad planning more than most cities — the queues at the big sights are genuinely long, and a poorly chosen hotel can put you 40 minutes from everything you came to see. Get the logistics right first and the rest of the trip takes care of itself.

Quick facts

Best time to visit
April–June or September–October (mild, fewer crowds than peak summer). August sees many small local businesses close for holidays.
Getting around
Metro covers the whole city; buy a Navigo Easy card and load individual tickets or a day pass onto it.
Minimum time needed
4 full days for the core sights, 6 with a day trip.
Rough daily budget
$50–75/day budget, $110–180/day mid-range, not counting flights — one of the pricier European capitals.

1. Which arrondissement to base yourself in

Paris is organized into 20 numbered arrondissements spiraling outward from the center. For a first visit, staying within arrondissements 1–9 keeps almost everything walkable or a short metro ride away:

  • Le Marais (3rd/4th) — historic, walkable, excellent food and boutique shopping, central to nearly everything.
  • Saint-Germain-des-Prés (6th) — classic Left Bank charm, cafes, close to the Louvre and Notre-Dame.
  • Montmartre (18th) — atmospheric hillside village feel, further from the center but with its own strong character and the Sacré-Cœur.

Avoid booking purely on price without checking the arrondissement — a cheap hotel in the far outer ring can cost you an hour a day in transit.

2. Skipping the lines that actually matter

Two queues can eat half a day if you don't plan around them:

  • The Louvre — book a timed-entry ticket online in advance, and enter through the Carrousel du Louvre underground entrance (via the Palais Royal–Musée du Louvre metro stop) rather than the main pyramid, where the line is usually much longer.
  • The Eiffel Tower — book tickets online for a specific time slot weeks ahead if you want to go up; walk-up lines for both tickets and security can run several hours in peak season. If you just want the view and photo, skip going up entirely and head to the Trocadéro esplanade across the river instead.
Pro tip The Louvre is free on the first Sunday of the month October–March, which sounds appealing but means significantly bigger crowds — go on a regular paid day if you actually want to see the art rather than the backs of people's heads.

3. Notre-Dame's reopening and Île de la Cité

Notre-Dame Cathedral reopened to the public in December 2024 following the 2019 fire and subsequent restoration. Entry is free but timed-entry tickets are strongly recommended given ongoing demand — check the cathedral's official site for current booking requirements before you go, as access rules have been updated periodically since reopening. Pair the visit with a walk around Île de la Cité and Sainte-Chapelle, whose stained glass is arguably more striking than Notre-Dame's interior.

4. Where Parisians actually eat

Skip the cafes with laminated multilingual menus directly facing major monuments — they're priced for one-time tourist traffic, not repeat local customers. Better signals:

  • A short, handwritten, French-only menu that changes with the season.
  • A boulangerie with a line of locals buying baguettes in the morning, not tourists buying croissants at noon.
  • Neighborhood bistros a few streets back from major sights, in the Marais or the 11th arrondissement especially.

A market visit is worth building into your trip — Marché des Enfants Rouges (Marais) or Marché Bastille (Thursday and Sunday mornings) for cheese, produce, and prepared food stalls.

5. A day trip that's genuinely worth it

Versailles, about 40 minutes by RER train, is the obvious choice and earns it — but go early and book a timed entry ticket, since the Hall of Mirrors gets extremely crowded by mid-morning. Budget the better part of a day if you also want to walk the gardens, which are enormous.

6. Common first-timer mistakes

  • Not booking timed tickets for the Louvre, Eiffel Tower, or Versailles in advance — walk-up queues at all three can run hours in peak season.
  • Assuming everywhere is walkable — Paris is bigger than the postcard version suggests; use the Metro for anything more than 20–25 minutes on foot.
  • Not learning a few basic French greetings — a simple "Bonjour" before asking a question, even in English, changes how you're received in shops and cafes.
  • Visiting in August expecting everything open — many independent restaurants and shops close for a few weeks; check ahead if a specific place is on your list.
