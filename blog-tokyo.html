Tokyo for First-Timers: A Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Guide

By Adrian Updated September 14, 2026 4 min read

Tokyo overwhelms people before they've even landed — the size of the map alone is intimidating. It stops being intimidating once you stop thinking of it as one city and start thinking of it as a cluster of very walkable neighborhoods connected by an excellent train system.

Quick facts

Best time to visit
Late March–April (cherry blossoms) or October–November (autumn colors). Both book up early.
Getting around
Suica or Pasmo IC card, tap in/out on JR, Tokyo Metro, and Toei lines. Google Maps handles routing well.
Minimum time needed
4 full days for the core neighborhoods, 6 with a day trip.
Rough daily budget
$50–70/day budget, $100–160/day mid-range, not counting flights — Tokyo is pricier than Southeast Asia but cheaper than most people expect for a global capital.

1. Where to base yourself

Pick a neighborhood on a major JR Yamanote Line stop and you can reach almost everything without transfers.

  • Shinjuku — best all-rounder: huge transit hub, endless food, nightlife, and Shinjuku Gyoen park nearby for a break from the density.
  • Shibuya — younger, trendier, closest to the famous crossing and Harajuku, slightly more nightlife-forward.
  • Asakusa — traditional, closest to Senso-ji Temple, calmer and more old-Tokyo in feel, but further from the Yamanote loop line.

First trip, short on time: Shinjuku or Shibuya. More time and want contrast: split your stay, a few nights each.

2. Do you actually need a rail pass?

The old advice to "always get the JR Pass" no longer holds for a Tokyo-only trip. The nationwide JR Pass is priced for people covering serious ground between cities (Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka, for example). If you're staying inside Tokyo, a rechargeable Suica or Pasmo IC card (tap in, tap out, works across JR, Metro, and Toei lines, and at convenience stores) is cheaper and far simpler.

Do the math the other way: if your trip includes a Shinkansen leg to another city, price a JR Pass against buying the individual tickets you'd actually take before committing — it's not automatically the better deal anymore.

Pro tip Load your IC card onto your phone's wallet app before you land if your phone supports it — one less physical card to manage, and you can top it up from your phone.

3. Asakusa: the traditional side

Senso-ji Temple, Tokyo's oldest temple, anchors Asakusa, with the Nakamise shopping street leading up to it lined with snack stalls and souvenir shops. It's genuinely touristy but earns the visit — go early morning to see it before the crowds, or evening after the shops close when the temple grounds are lit and much quieter.

4. Shibuya & Harajuku: the modern side

Shibuya Crossing is worth seeing once, ideally from above — the Shibuya Sky observation deck or one of the cafes overlooking the intersection gives you the view without just standing in the middle of it with everyone else's camera. From there, it's a short walk to Harajuku's Takeshita Street for the youth-fashion, crepe-stand chaos, and Meiji Jingu, a genuinely peaceful forested shrine a few minutes away — one of the sharpest contrasts you'll find within a 10-minute walk anywhere in the city.

5. Where locals actually eat

Skip the sit-down "tourist course" restaurants near major stations and look instead for:

  • Standing ramen and soba counters near train stations — order from a vending machine at the door, hand the ticket to staff, eat, leave. Fast, cheap, and how a lot of Tokyo actually eats lunch.
  • Depachika (department store basement food halls) — Isetan Shinjuku and Mitsukoshi Ginza have excellent, affordable prepared-food counters for a picnic-style meal.
  • Tsukiji Outer Market — the old fish market's outer retail street remains open and is a genuinely good breakfast crawl: fresh sushi, grilled skewers, and tamagoyaki stalls, best visited before 9am.

6. One modern, one traditional day trip

  • Kamakura (under an hour by train) — coastal town, the Great Buddha statue, and a genuinely good hiking trail between temples. Easy half-day or full-day trip.
  • Nikko (about two hours by train) — elaborate shrine complexes set in mountain forest, a bigger day and worth an early start.

If you want something distinctly modern rather than historic, teamLab's digital art museum (there are multiple locations; check which is currently open before booking) and Akihabara's electronics-and-anime district both fill a half-day well and need advance tickets or at least a plan, since they get busy.

7. Common first-timer mistakes

  • Not carrying enough cash — Tokyo has modernized fast, but plenty of small restaurants, shrines, and older shops are still cash-only.
  • Overpacking the itinerary — transit is fast and easy, which tempts people into cramming five neighborhoods into one day. Two well-paced areas a day beats a rushed five.
  • Skipping reservations for teamLab or popular restaurants — Tokyo's most in-demand spots do sell out days or weeks ahead, unlike a lot of casual dining elsewhere in Asia.
  • Eating and walking, or eating on trains — both are considered poor etiquette in most of Japan; step to the side of a street stall or sit down to eat instead.
