Seoul for First-Timers: Palaces, Hongdae & the T-money Card

By Axel Updated September 14, 2026 11 min read

Seoul runs on contrast — 600-year-old palaces a ten-minute walk from convenience stores selling instant ramen and skincare masks, hanok villages backed by glass towers. It rewards a loose plan more than a tight one; leave room to wander.

Quick facts

Best time to visit
April (cherry blossoms) or October–November (autumn foliage). Both are peak season — book ahead.
Getting around
T-money card, tap in/out on subway and buses — one of the most efficient metro systems anywhere.
Minimum time needed
4 full days, 5–6 with a DMZ or Nami Island day trip.
Rough daily budget
$40–60/day budget, $90–140/day mid-range, not counting flights.

1. Where to base yourself

  • Myeongdong — central, shopping and skincare shops everywhere, walkable to several major sights, can feel a bit touristy at street level.
  • Hongdae — university-district energy, nightlife, street performances on weekends, younger crowd.
  • Ikseon-dong / Jongno — hanok-lined alleys turned into cafes and boutiques, closest to the main palaces, more character.

2. Gyeongbokgung and the hanbok rental thing

Gyeongbokgung Palace is Seoul's largest and most important royal palace, and the main gate's changing-of-the-guard ceremony (held several times daily) is worth timing your visit around. The genuinely fun local tradition: rent a hanbok (traditional Korean dress) from one of the many shops surrounding the palace — wearing one gets you free entry to several palaces, and the photos against the palace architecture are the best souvenir you'll bring home.

Pro tip Rent your hanbok as early in the day as you can manage — the light is better for photos before midday, and the palace grounds get significantly more crowded by early afternoon.

3. Bukchon Hanok Village: go early or go home

Bukchon Hanok Village, a neighborhood of traditional hanok houses between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces, is genuinely beautiful — and also a residential area where people actually live. Visit before 9am for quiet streets and good light; by mid-morning, tour groups fill the main photo-spot alleys, and residents (understandably) have asked visitors to keep noise down, which most guides posted locally now explicitly note.

4. Myeongdong and Hongdae: shopping vs. nightlife

Myeongdong is Seoul's most concentrated shopping and street-food strip — Korean skincare brands, styling tools, and street snacks (tteokbokki, hotteok, giant fried chicken skewers) on every block. Hongdae, near the university, is the better pick after dark — live buskers, noraebang (karaoke rooms), and a much younger nightlife scene than Myeongdong's shopping-first energy.

5. Korean BBQ etiquette worth knowing

Korean barbecue is a highlight for most first-time visitors, and a few etiquette basics go a long way: let staff or the eldest at the table handle the grill if you're unsure, use the provided scissors (not a knife) to cut meat, and wrap grilled meat in lettuce with rice and ssamjang (fermented chili paste) rather than eating it plain. Side dishes (banchan) are free and refillable — don't be shy about asking for more.

6. Is the DMZ tour worth it?

The DMZ (Demilitarized Zone) tour to the Korean border is one of Seoul's most-booked day trips, and opinions are genuinely split. It's a heavily guided, somewhat scripted experience with strict rules (passport required, dress code, no filming in some sections) — worth it if you're interested in modern Korean history and the ongoing division, less essential if you're short on time and more drawn to the city itself. Book in advance; some tour segments sell out days ahead, and access rules change periodically, so confirm current requirements before booking.

7. Common first-timer mistakes

  • Not getting a T-money card immediately — sold at any convenience store near a subway station, works on subway and bus with a transfer discount.
  • Skipping reservations for popular restaurants — some well-known spots (especially in Myeongdong and Hongdae) do turn people away without one.
  • Visiting Bukchon Hanok Village loudly at midday — it's a residential neighborhood; keep noise down and respect "no entry" signs on private alleys.
  • Assuming everywhere takes cash — Seoul is heavily card- and app-based; some smaller vendors no longer accept cash at all.
